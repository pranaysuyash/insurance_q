# ASSUMPTIONS THE BUILDER TREATS AS OBVIOUS (THAT I FIND STRANGE)

**1. That the audience wants an English-language name at all.**
The brief says "Indian households, many languages." Then every candidate on the slate — *North & Kin, Held Together, Avylo, Keralune, Toveli* — is English, and several are English that even educated English speakers would stumble over. To a Tamil grandmother, or a Hindi-speaking father in Gorakhpur, *Avylo* and *Keralune* sound like pharma startups or crypto tokens. They carry zero pre-loaded meaning. The builder has decided, without evidence, that the brand must live in English even though the product's entire reason to exist is that the documents are in an English these families don't fully read. Why is the *name* allowed to be opaque when the whole product is about removing opacity?

**2. That "warm, calm, competent" is the right emotional register.**
Families open insurance documents at three moments: a death, a hospital bill, a denied claim, a renewal they can't afford. They are not calm. They are anxious, suspicious, and often grieving. "Calm" is the builder projecting their own afternoon onto the user. What the user actually wants is not calm — it is *concrete reassurance that this thing will tell them, in their language, what they are owed.* "Held Together" is a meditation app name. The audience does not want to be held. They want to be paid, and to understand the paper that says whether they will be.

**3. That naming the category is a rookie mistake.**
The doctrine bans insurance words. But in India, the category word *bima* / *vima* (बीमा) is universally understood across Hindi, Marathi, Bengali, Gujarati, Kannada, and is the actual word families say when they talk about this. *PolicyBazaar* put "Policy" in the name and became a unicorn. *Bajaj Allianz*, *LIC* — the trusted giants name the category openly. Hiding the category is a Silicon Valley instinct (Apple stopped saying "computer") borrowed by a founder who has no marketing budget to teach people what *Norial* means. When trust is low and literacy around the product is low, naming the category is not unsophisticated — it is the cheapest comprehension you can buy.

**4. That coined/arbitrary marks are "more ownable."**
They are more ownable *as trademarks.* They are catastrophically expensive *as meaning.* A startup called *Toveli* must spend crores to teach the market that it has anything to do with insurance. A startup called something with *bima* in it is understood in the first half-second, for free. The builder is optimizing for the trademark registry and forgetting the cognition cost. For a bootstrapped India-first app, comprehension is more expensive than legal clearance.

**5. That abstract outcomes beat concrete functions.**
*All There, In Order, Good Measure, Long View.* These could name a yoga app, a closet organizer, a financial-planning tool for the rich, or a marriage-counseling service. The builder assumes abstract = premium. But first-time digital-finance users in India trust the *specific* over the abstract. They trust "this will read my policy and tell me what it covers in Hindi" more than "your life, held together." Premium vagueness reads, to a semi-urban household, as evasion.

# WHEN "NAME THE OUTCOME" IS ACTUALLY WRONG

- **When the audience cannot imagine the outcome.** The doctrine assumes the user already knows what a good insurance-explainer feels like, and you are naming that feeling. But these families have *never* had an insurance document explained to them. The outcome is not in their mental model. You are naming something that does not exist for them. Naming the *function* (read, explain, translate, reckon) names something they can already want.
- **When the category itself is the trust signal.** In finance, "this is a bank" is the value, not the liability. Hiding the category hides the trust.
- **When the local language already carries pre-loaded trust vocabulary for the domain.** Ignoring *bima, suraksha, vishwas, sahi, hisaab* is leaving decades of cultural trust on the table to chase a Western-brand-school aesthetic.
- **When the user is anxious, not aspirational.** Outcomes are aspirational framing. Anxious people want "this solves the thing in my hand, right now."

# THE DIRECTION THE SLATE COMPLETELY IGNORES

**Indic-rooted, concrete-functional names that don't try to be clever.** Names that sound like they could be a cooperative society, a post-office scheme, or a trusted relative — not a Y Combinator company. Names that work as the *first line of translation* the product offers. The builder's slate is optimizing for a Western VC's taste; this direction optimizes for a Vadodara father who has a LIC term plan and a hospital bill. Every name on the current slate fails the "say it to your uncle test." The names below pass it.

# PITCHES (FROM THE IGNORED DIRECTION)

### 1. Sahaj (सहज)
- **Pronunciation:** sa-HAJ
- **Type:** Real Sanskrit/Hindi word; suggestive mark. Means *natural, easy, effortless, without effort.*
- **Descriptor:** *Sahaj — aapka bima, aasaan bhasha mein* (your insurance, in easy language).
- **Builder would reject it because:** it's a common dictionary word (ownability nightmare), it isn't English, it isn't "premium," and there are already big Indian brands using "Sahaj" (Sahaj e-Village is a major rural govt-services network).
- **Audience might love it because:** the word *already carries the emotional promise the product makes* — effortless, natural, not a fight. It sounds like a benevolent institution, not a startup. Every Hindi/Bengali/Marathi speaker reads it and feels the meaning for free.
- **What breaks it:** trademark is genuinely crowded (Sahaj e-Village, Sahaj Solar, many others). May feel "government-y" to urban elites. Weak in South India where the Sanskrit echo is fainter.
- **Collision/cultural risk:** real and serious on trademarks; low cultural risk (the word is universally warm).

### 2. Samjho (समझो)
- **Pronunciation:** sum-JHO
- **Type:** Imperative verb — *understand.* Real Hindi/Urdu word.
- **Descriptor:** *Samjho — aapki policy, aapki bhasha mein* (understand — your policy, in your language).
- **Builder would reject it because:** it's an instruction, not an outcome; it's a common verb (essentially un-ownable as a standalone mark); it's Hindi-only; it sounds like an NGO campaign or a government slogan, not a product.
- **Audience might love it because:** it is the exact action they want to perform on the document. It is direct, human, and feels like a helpful relative, not a corporation. The verb *is* the value prop.
- **What breaks it:** cannot be trademarked alone — needs a lockup (*Samjho Bima, Samjho Policy*). Won't land natively in non-Hindi regions without per-language localization (Tamil *puṇar*, Telugu *artham Chesuko*, etc.).
- **Collision/cultural risk:** imperatives can land as scolding in some registers ("samjho" can mean "listen up / be reasonable"). Tone-sensitive.

### 3. BimaBol (बीमाबोल)
- **Pronunciation:** BEE-ma-bol
- **Type:** Coinage from two real words — *bima* (insurance) + *bol* (speak/say). Compound suggestive mark.
- **Descriptor:** *BimaBol — your insurance, finally speaking your language.*
- **Builder would reject it because:** it contains an insurance word (violates the spirit of the doctrine even though "bima" is not on the English banned-token list), it's a compound pun, and the doctrine explicitly hates cleverness. It also leans Hindi-belt.
- **Audience might love it because:** it names the category in the first syllable (instant comprehension across most of India) and names the function in the second (the document *talks to you*). It is ownable as a compound. And *bol* is one of the most democratic, warm verbs in Indian languages — it's what you say to a person, not a document.
- **What breaks it:** wordplay can age badly; "bima + bol" may read as gimmicky to urban elites; in the South the native word is *vima*, so you'd need *VimaBol* regionally.
- **Collision/cultural risk:** low-to-moderate on trademarks; the bigger risk is the Hindi-centric framing in a "many languages" market — but "bima" is nationally understood via LIC and government usage, so it travels further than the builder assumes.

### 4. Hisaab (हिसाब)
- **Pronunciation:** hi-SAAB
- **Type:** Real word, Persian-rooted, used across Hindi, Urdu, Marathi, Gujarati, Punjabi, Bengali. Means *account, reckoning, calculation, settling up.*
- **Descriptor:** *Hisaab — know what your policy actually owes you.*
- **Builder would reject it because:** it's a common word, un-ownable alone, doesn't name the category, and "hisaab" is associated with accounting/bookkeeping (too functional, not warm enough).
- **Audience might love it because:** *hisaab* is the word Indian families already use for the moment of reckoning — "hisaab barabar karna" (settle the account). Insurance, for them, is precisely a *hisaab* problem: will the numbers settle in my favor? The word names the actual anxiety.
- **What breaks it:** generic on its own; needs a lockup (*Hisaab Bima, Bima Hisaab*). May feel too transactional to the "warm/calm" camp.
- **Collision/cultural risk:** dozens of "Hisaab" apps exist (bookkeeping tools); trademark is dense. Cultural risk is near-zero — the word is loved.

### 5. Sahi (सही)
- **Pronunciation:** sa-HEE
- **Type:** Real word; evaluative adjective. Means *correct, right, true, authentic* in Hindi/Urdu.
- **Descriptor:** *Sahi — get your policy right.*
- **Builder would reject it because:** un-ownable common adjective, no outcome named, generic, used by everyone.
- **Audience might love it because:** *sahi* is arguably the single most-used trust word in Indian advertising and commerce (*sahi daam, sahi price, sahi khiladi*). The entire promise of insurance advice collapses to one question: *is this sahi?* The word is the answer.
- **What breaks it:** extremely crowded trademarks; works best as a lockup (*Sahi Bima, Sahi Cover* — though "cover" is banned).
- **Collision/cultural risk:** high on trademarks, essentially nil culturally.

# THE THING MOST PEOPLE MISS ABOUT THIS

The builder is treating "many languages" as a *localization* problem to solve *after* naming the app in English. But the choice of an English coined name is itself a language choice, and it positions the app, silently, as English-first, urban-first, educated-first — which is *exactly the barrier that made the insurance documents incomprehensible to these families in the first place.* The name is the first sentence of the product. If the first sentence is in a language the user doesn't speak, the product has already failed its own thesis before the user opens it. An Indic-rooted name is not a "regional" decision — it is the product's first feature: the first line of the translation it promises to deliver. Naming it *Avylo* tells the family "this is for someone who reads English." Naming it *Samjho* or *BimaBol* tells them "this is for you." That is not a branding preference. It is the entire product, stated in one word.
