# DEVIL'S ADVOCATE — CoverWise Rename

## 1. The contrarian case: the founder is over-thinking this

- **Ship-first reality check.** Zero users, zero revenue, solo portfolio project. A name at 0→1 only needs to clear two bars: not sued, and pronounceable. Both are an afternoon's work. "North & Kin" or "Kitchen Drawer" will not move a single install. The actual P0 is whether the OCR + source-cited summary + citation pipeline works on a real LIC policy PDF scanned on a 2019 Redmi. That's what determines survival. Naming is a P3 problem wearing P1 clothes.
- **App-store search is won by industry words, not outcome words.** Open the Play Store in India and search like a real human: "insurance policy", "policy summary", "lic policy check", "health insurance tracker". Every top hit has *insurance* or *policy* in the literal app name. Search "All There" or "Held Together" — you get a Taylor Swift lyric, a self-help book, and a hymn. The doctrine's ban on industry tokens is a self-inflicted gunshot to discoverability. The user at 2am in a hospital does not search for "stewardship."
- **Portfolio-reusability is a solution to a problem that does not exist.** There is no portfolio. There is one half-built app. Choosing a name flexible enough to also sell warranties and B2B distribution is a founder buying a 500-seat org chart on day one. If a second product ever ships, you rename then — with real users and real data. Premature portfolio-thinking is exactly how you end up at "Avylo": flexible enough to mean anything, which means it means nothing.
- **The naming exercise is displacing launch work.** Every hour spent scoring "Norial vs Toveli vs Keralune" (all coined, all unmemorable, all pre-rejected by the founder's own gut) is an hour not spent on citation accuracy, renewal-reminder reliability, or the family-access flow that decides whether the first user trusts the app on the worst day of their life. The 16-name slate is the evidence: this is a person doing comfortable, iterative, low-stakes work to avoid uncomfortable, binary, high-stakes work.
- **The 10/100 CoverWise score is misread.** It does not mean "the brand is broken." It means "the brand sits in a contested category." The fix is not a coined masterbrand — it is a name so bluntly, searchably, unambiguously about reading insurance documents that no user mistypes it and no competitor wants it. The doctrine treats "descriptive" as weakness; in app-store reality, descriptive IS the moat.

## 2. Challenging the four premises

**Premise 1 — "needs a defensible ownable masterbrand."** Wrong at this stage. Defensibility comes from trademark registration plus product traction, neither of which requires a coined mark. "Descriptive + registered + SEO-dominant" is a more durable moat for a 0-user app than "coined + unregistered + undiscoverable." A masterbrand is a downstream asset you earn, not a launch input you decree.

**Premise 2 — "name the outcome, not the industry."** Wrong for India insurance specifically. The outcome is invisible until a crisis. The industry term is what the user types in panic. An outcome-name ("All There", "Held Together") requires the user to already know the brand exists before they need it — which means a paid-acquisition dependency, which means higher CAC, which kills a solo founder's economics. Industry-word discovery is free distribution. Outcome-word brand is paid distribution. The doctrine optimizes for the expensive one.

**Premise 3 — "portfolio-reusable across future products."** Internally inconsistent. Ownable marks are specific. Reusable marks are vague. You cannot maximize both, and the rejected slate (Norial, Toveli, Keralune, Avylo) is what happens when you try — flexible marks that mean nothing to anyone. The roadmap has zero funded second products. This premise is paying real launch cost to preserve hypothetical optionality.

**Premise 4 — "coined/arbitrary/suggestive > descriptive."** Wrong on the evidence for app-store categories. Top Indian insurance apps (PolicyBazaar, Coverfox, Digit, Acko, Plum) are descriptive or arbitrary-but-searchable. The one true coin — Acko — spent nine figures to make the coin mean "insurance." Coined marks require marketing capital this founder does not have. For a portfolio app with no ad budget, descriptive is the capital-efficient choice. The trademark-strength argument is real but slow — it pays off in year 7, not year 1. There is no year-7 money.

## 3. Concession

The doctrine would be right if:
(a) the founder had a marketing budget north of ₹5Cr/year to brute-force awareness of a coined name;
(b) the primary distribution channel were PR / word-of-mouth / referral, not Play Store search;
(c) the second and third products were already funded and on the roadmap with named teams;
(d) the category were novel enough that users would search for the brand name itself rather than the category (true for CRED or Razorpay — not for "insurance document reader");
(e) the founder's instinct for coined names were reliably good — the rejected slate (Norial, Toveli, Keralune, Avylo) suggests it is not.

If any three of those five were true, the doctrine wins. None are.

## 4. Contrarian pitches

### PITCH 1 — Policy Reader

- **Pronunciation:** PAH-luh-see REE-der. Hindi-English: पॉलिसी रीडर. Two plain English words; zero spelling ambiguity; voice-to-text safe across Hindi/Tamil/Gujarati/Marathi accents.
- **Type:** Descriptive / function-literal. Uses the banned token *policy* as a deliberate control token — justification below.
- **Descriptor:** "Policy Reader — Understand every insurance policy your family owns."
- **Why contrarian wins here:** Owns the exact verb the app performs and the exact noun the user searches. ASO: ranks for "policy reader", "read my insurance policy", "insurance policy summary", "lic policy reader" — search strings real Indians type. Function IS the positioning; no marketing spend required to explain what it does. The very blandness that makes it a weak trademark (the doctrine's objection) is what makes it a strong discovery asset (the contrarian's counter). For a solo founder with no ad budget, free discovery beats trademark strength in year 7. Deliberate control-token use of "policy" is justified because the user's mental sentence is literally "I can't read my policy" — naming anything else is naming around the user.
- **What breaks it:** Descriptive marks are the hardest to register in Class 9/42 in India — likely needs a disclaimer amendment and years of supplementary-protection evidence. A competitor can launch "Quick Policy Reader" or "Policy Reader Pro" and you have weak opposition grounds. "Reader" undersells the gap-finding / renewal-tracking / family-access intelligence — the name implies passive consumption, which may cap perceived value. Still single-category-locked, failing the portfolio test — but per the contrarian case, that test is premature.

### PITCH 2 — Insurance Khata

- **Pronunciation:** in-SHOO-runs KHAA-taa (खाता). Two words, both already in the Indian household vocabulary.
- **Type:** Descriptive + household-object metaphor. Uses the banned token *insurance* as a deliberate control token; *khata* is a real, common household word — not fabricated etymology, not clever coinage, not fake Sanskrit.
- **Descriptor:** "Insurance Khata — Your family's protection, finally written down clear."
- **Why contrarian wins here:** *Khata* (bahi-khata) is the household ledger that every Indian family — across Hindi, Marathi, Gujarati, Tamil, Bengali — already owns, already trusts, and already understands as "the place where we record what's ours." It is the single most universal household-object metaphor in the country, and it is sitting there untrademarked. The doctrine says "name the outcome, not the industry" — but the user's actual mental model is "I want my insurance written down in one place I can find." Khata IS that outcome, in the user's own language, not in a strategist's deck. SEO: "insurance khata" / "policy khata" is a uniquely ownable Indian-English string no global insurer will ever claim. Localization-proof — khata has cognates in every Indian language, so the brand survives translation, which "North & Kin" and "All There" do not. Warm, competent, calm — matches the brief without trying.
- **What breaks it:** Two words, harder to render as a clean wordmark. "Khata" carries a bookkeeping/accounting connotation that may read as dry rather than warm-protection — though for the crisis user (death, claim rejection), "the book where everything is recorded" is precisely what they want. The English-urban elite may perceive it as "too vernacular," which could cap the premium B2B-distribution ambition. "Insurance" front-loads the industry the doctrine wanted to escape — which is the entire contrarian point, but will be the first objection in the room. Still fails portfolio-reusability — which is the point.

## The thing most people miss about this:

The naming doctrine is optimizing for the wrong failure mode. It is built to avoid "we picked a name that became a liability" (the CoverWise → CoverSure collision). But the far more common failure mode for solo-founder India-first apps is **"we picked a name that nobody ever typed into a search bar."** The first failure is visible, painful, and gets all the attention. The second is invisible — it surfaces 18 months later as a "retention problem" or a "product-market fit problem" when it is actually a discoverability leak at the top of the funnel. The 16-name slate will be re-litigated in 18 months not because the names are bad but because none of them are searchable, and the founder will blame retention when the real leak is acquisition. The honest rename question is not "what is ownable" — it is **"what will a man in a hospital at 2am type into the Play Store while his mother is being admitted."** That is the only user, the only moment, and the only search that matters. Every name in the curated slate fails that test.
